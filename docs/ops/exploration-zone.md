## 1. Exploration Zone framing for Gale crater and Mount Sharp

NASA's human landing site process calls a candidate location where humans could land, live and work on Mars an Exploration Zone: "a collection of Regions of Interest (ROIs) that are located within approximately 100 kilometers of a centralized landing site", where ROIs are "areas that are relevant for scientific investigation and/or development/maturation of capabilities and resources necessary for a sustainable human presence" (S1). Gale crater was put forward as such a site at the First Landing Site/Exploration Zone Workshop (Houston, 27–30 October 2015) in the abstract "Assessing Gale Crater as a Landing Site for the First Human Mission to Mars", which proposes a 100 km radius EZ with science and resource ROIs, and names Mount Sharp's "5 km sedimentary stack" among the site's science attractions and water bound in minerals and adsorbed to loose grains as a possible water source, while noting "little chance of near-surface water-ice" (S2). Our site is the Mount Sharp boxwork region of Gale ([ADR-001](../decisions/ADR-001-site-selection.md); AOI in [aoi.md](feasibility/aoi.md)); there Curiosity found boxwork, crisscrossing low ridges that NASA reports likely formed when groundwater left minerals in rock cracks, suggesting groundwater persisted underground as the surface dried (S3). We therefore present the AOI as one EZ whose stops are science, resource and engineering regions of interest (01 §A.4). This is our own framing built on NASA's terminology: S2 is a 2015 community proposal, not a NASA selection. S1 names scientific and resource ROIs and speaks of "capabilities"; it does not name an engineering ROI type, so `engineering` is the team's own label.

## 2. Stop-type list

The three stop types are exactly those of 01 FR-45 and 05 `stop_type`: `science | resource | engineering`. T04.04 and T04.05 set `stop_type`, `ez_theme`, the objective (`science_objective`, at most 300 characters) and `evidence` (at least one `{text, source_id}`) on every POI. Evidence text paraphrases a cited page only; positions, sols and dates come from the cited traverse or image page, never from this note.

| `stop_type` | Meaning (one line) | How a POI gets this type, its objective and its evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `science` | A region of interest for scientific investigation (S1), at Gale for example the groundwater record in boxwork (S3) or the layers of Mount Sharp (S2) | Use when the POI's objective is to read a geological record (layers, boxwork ridges). `ez_theme` examples: "climate history" and "groundwater" (01 §A.4 labels). Objective: one sentence from the cited NASA page. Evidence: the mission page or image page that shows the feature, for example the boxwork article (S3) for a boxwork ridge. |
| `resource` | A region of interest for resources a sustainable human presence would need (S1), at Gale water bound in minerals or adsorbed to loose grains, and feedstock elements (S2) | Use when the POI's objective is a candidate in-situ resource. `ez_theme` example: "hydrated minerals" (01 §A.4 stop-type label; the 05 example is "Hydrated sulfates — ISRU water"). Evidence: a cited measurement or assessment, for example (S2) for sand and mineral-bound water as a possible source and for Fe, Al and Si feedstock regions identified from ChemCam results. The `evidence` text must say that S2 is a proposal and that near-surface ice is unlikely there (S2 says "little chance"; do not copy 01's "no shallow ice" as a sourced claim). At least one resource POI must be in the demo plan (FR-45 acceptance). |
| `engineering` | A region relevant to how a crew and vehicles operate on the terrain (team definition; 01 §A.4 lists slopes, loose fines and rock fields near the hab) | Use when the POI's objective is trafficability or an infrastructure candidate. `ez_theme` example: "terrain" (01 §A.4 label). Evidence: the team's derived layers (slope, thermal inertia, with their own source ids) and rover scout images; (S2) notes that soil mechanics, traversability and rock density are measured in situ rather than resolved by orbital assets, and identifies small crater "camps" as infrastructure points. S1 names scientific and resource ROIs and speaks of "capabilities"; it does not name an engineering ROI type, so `engineering` is the team's own label; see open items. |

## 3. Sources

S1. Bussey, B. and Hoffman, S. J. (paper abstract, 2016 IEEE Aerospace Conference, "Human Mars Landing Site and Impacts on Mars Surface Operations"; NTRS record authors field; presented 5–12 March 2016, Big Sky, MT). NASA Technical Reports Server, document 20150013826. URL: <https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20150013826/downloads/20150013826.pdf>. Accessed 2026-11-09 (text extracted from the PDF).

- Supports: "NASA has begun a process to identify and discuss candidate locations where humans could land, live and work on the martian surface. These locations are referred to as Exploration Zones (EZs). Given current mission concepts, an EZ is a collection of Regions of Interest (ROIs) that are located within approximately 100 kilometers of a centralized landing site. ROIs are areas that are relevant for scientific investigation and/or development/maturation of capabilities and resources necessary for a sustainable human presence."

S1b. Bussey, B. and Davis, R., First Landing Site/Exploration Zone Workshop for Human Missions to the Surface of Mars, First Announcement (version 7.8), October 27–30, 2015, signed by them as co-chairs of the Human Landing Sites Study Steering Committee. URL: <https://www.nasa.gov/sites/default/files/atoms/files/first-landing-site-announcement.pdf>. Accessed 2026-11-09 (text extracted from the PDF).

- Supports: the same Exploration Zone and ROI wording as S1, and "The EZ also contains a landing site and a habitation site that will be used by multiple human crews during missions to explore and utilize the ROIs within the EZ." and "(a) identifying locations that would maximize the potential science return from future human exploration missions, (b) identifying locations with the potential for resources required to support humans".

S2. Calef, F. J. III, Archer, D., Clark, B., Day, M., Goetz, W., Lasue, J., Martin-Torres, J. and Zorzano Mier, M. (2015). "Assessing Gale Crater as a Landing Site for the First Human Mission to Mars." Abstract 1020, First Landing Site/Exploration Zone Workshop for Human Missions to the Surface of Mars, LPI Contribution No. 1879, Lunar and Planetary Institute, Houston. URL: <https://www.hou.usra.edu/meetings/explorationzone2015/pdf/1020.pdf>. Accessed 2026-11-09 (text extracted from the PDF). Bibliographic record: <https://abdn.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/assessing-gale-crater-as-a-landing-site-for-the-first-human-missi/>.

- Supports the proposal: "Besides the nominal 100 km radius Exploration Zone (EZ, in red), we've also identified a smaller 25 km radius EZ (pink) to emphasize the scientific and ISRU ROIs closer to the landing site."
- Supports science: "the yet fully explored 5 km sedimentary stack of Mt. Sharp (Aeolis Mons) are a scientific cornucopia for an human mission to expand upon and explore".
- Supports resource: "There is little chance of near-surface water-ice for resource utilization in Gale, but water bound in minerals and adsorbed to loose grains could serve as a water source" and "Using geochemical results from the CHEMCAM instrument on MSL, we've identified an ROIs for Al, Fe, and Si".
- Supports engineering: "the insitu observations of rock density, soil mechanics, temperature fluctuations, dust opacity, radiation (via Curiosity), traversability, not to mention insitu science, are not readily measureable or resolvable by orbital assets" and "Several 'camps' were also identified within small ~100 m diameter craters ... that could serve as infrastructure points within a few kilometers of the landing site".
- Limit: the proposal states its own landing-site position, which is not our AOI; it is a community abstract, not a NASA decision.

S3. NASA/JPL (23 June 2025). "NASA's Curiosity Mars Rover Starts Unpacking Boxwork Formations." URL: <https://www.nasa.gov/missions/mars-science-laboratory/curiosity-rover/nasas-curiosity-mars-rover-starts-unpacking-boxwork-formations/>. Accessed 2026-11-09 (page text via fetch tool, to be re-read by the checker).

- Supports: boxwork is "crisscrossing low ridges, some just a few inches tall, arranged in what geologists call a boxwork pattern"; the bedrock below these ridges "likely formed when groundwater trickling through the rock left behind minerals that accumulated in those cracks and fissures, hardening and becoming cementlike"; "Eons of sandblasting by Martian wind wore away the rock but not the minerals"; the patterns "show that even in the midst of this drying, water was still present underground"; the newly drilled region has features that "could reveal" whether Mars' subsurface once provided an environment suitable for life; Curiosity drilled a rock nicknamed "Altadena" and plans to drill more samples from larger ridges farther away.

S4. Davis, J. (29 October 2015). "At Mars Workshop, Science and Human Spaceflight Find Common Ground." The Planetary Society. URL: <https://www.planetary.org/articles/20151029-mars-science-hsf-common-ground>. Accessed 2026-11-09 (page text via fetch tool, to be re-read by the checker). Context only, not used for a claim above: reports that "about 45 Martian 'exploration zones'" were pitched, with resource ROIs labelled RROIs and science ROIs SROIs (diagram caption for Mawrth Vallis).

S5. NASA, Mars Science Laboratory (Curiosity) mission page. URL: <https://science.nasa.gov/mission/msl-curiosity/>. Accessed 2026-11-09 (page text via fetch tool). Context only: lists landing on Aug. 6, 2012 in Gale Crater and the mission goal question "Did Mars ever have the right environmental conditions to support small life forms called microbes?".
